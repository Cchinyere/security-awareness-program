# Executive Security Awareness

## Purpose

Executives and senior leaders are high-value targets because they have authority, access to sensitive information, influence over business decisions, and visibility across the organization.

This training helps executives understand their role in reducing cyber risk, supporting security culture, and making effective decisions during security incidents.

## Key Risks

Executives are exposed to risks such as:

- Executive phishing
- Business email compromise
- Impersonation
- Deepfake audio or video fraud
- Sensitive data exposure
- Credential theft
- Travel-related cyber risks
- Unapproved use of AI tools
- Requests to bypass normal controls
- Reputational and regulatory impact after incidents

## Why Executives Are Targeted

Attackers may target executives because they can:

- Approve payments
- Influence urgent decisions
- Access confidential business information
- Request exceptions to normal processes
- Be impersonated to pressure employees
- Hold accounts that attackers can use for internal fraud

## Required Behaviours

Executives should:

1. Model good security behaviour.
2. Use MFA and approved authentication methods consistently.
3. Avoid bypassing security controls for convenience.
4. Verify unusual payment, data, or access requests.
5. Protect confidential board, financial, and strategic information.
6. Use only approved tools for sensitive business communication.
7. Report suspicious emails, calls, messages, or account activity.
8. Support a no-blame reporting culture.
9. Reinforce security as a business risk, not only an IT issue.

## AI-Era Considerations

Executives should understand that attackers may use AI to create:

- Deepfake video calls
- Voice-cloned instructions
- Highly personalized phishing emails
- Fake investor, supplier, or regulator messages
- Synthetic media that creates false urgency
- Fraudulent instructions appearing to come from senior leaders

Executives should not rely on voice, video, writing style, or apparent authority alone as proof of identity.

## High-Risk Executive Scenarios

Extra caution is required when dealing with:

- Urgent payment instructions
- Bank detail changes
- Confidential document requests
- Legal or regulatory communications
- Merger, acquisition, or investment discussions
- Requests to approve access exceptions
- Instructions received while travelling
- Messages asking for passwords, MFA codes, or account recovery support

## Verification Protocol

For high-risk requests:

1. Pause before acting.
2. Verify using trusted contact details already held by the organization.
3. Use a second communication channel where appropriate.
4. Require dual approval for payments or sensitive changes.
5. Avoid using contact details supplied in the suspicious message.
6. Report suspected impersonation immediately.

## Leadership Responsibilities

Executives should support the awareness program by:

- Endorsing security awareness campaigns.
- Participating in executive cyber briefings.
- Supporting security policies and controls.
- Encouraging staff to report mistakes early.
- Allocating resources for cyber risk reduction.
- Participating in incident response exercises.
- Treating security as part of business resilience.

## Incident Response Role

During a cyber incident, executives may need to:

- Support timely decision-making.
- Approve communication strategies.
- Engage legal, regulatory, or public relations teams.
- Balance operational continuity with containment.
- Avoid unapproved external communication.
- Follow the incident response structure.
- Preserve evidence and confidentiality.

## Travel Security

Executives should apply additional care when travelling:

- Avoid public Wi-Fi for sensitive work unless using approved protection.
- Keep devices physically secure.
- Avoid discussing sensitive matters in public spaces.
- Report lost or stolen devices immediately.
- Use approved communication platforms.
- Be cautious of unexpected meeting links or travel-related messages.

## AI Tool Use

Executives should not enter the following into unapproved AI tools:

- Board papers
- Financial forecasts
- Personal data
- Customer information
- Legal advice
- Acquisition or strategy documents
- Incident details
- Confidential business plans

AI-generated outputs should be reviewed before use in decision-making.

## Scenario Examples

### Scenario 1: Voice-Cloned Payment Request

A finance employee receives a call that sounds like a senior executive asking for an urgent payment.

Expected response:

- The employee should verify through a trusted channel.
- The executive should support this verification culture.
- No one should be penalized for challenging unusual requests.

### Scenario 2: Executive Email Compromise

An executive notices emails they did not send in their sent folder.

Expected response:

- Report immediately.
- Do not delete evidence.
- Change credentials only through approved IT support.
- Support investigation and communication steps.

### Scenario 3: Confidential Data Request

A message appears to come from a board member requesting sensitive documents urgently.

Expected response:

- Verify the identity and business need.
- Use approved secure sharing methods.
- Escalate if the request is unusual.

## Metrics

Recommended metrics include:

- Executive training completion rate
- Executive participation in tabletop exercises
- Number of executive impersonation attempts reported
- Time taken to report suspicious executive account activity
- Compliance with high-risk approval workflows
- Completion of AI acceptable use training

## Key Takeaways

- Executives are high-value cyber targets.
- Leadership behaviour shapes security culture.
- Deepfakes and voice cloning increase impersonation risk.
- Verification is a strength, not a lack of trust.
- Cybersecurity is a business resilience issue.
