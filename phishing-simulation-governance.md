# Phishing Simulation Governance

## Purpose

This document provides governance guidance for running phishing simulations in a safe, ethical, and educational way.

Phishing simulations should not be used to embarrass, punish, or shame employees. They should help the organization understand user risk, improve reporting behaviour, strengthen controls, and provide targeted awareness support.

## Objectives

The objectives of phishing simulation activities are to:

1. Improve employee ability to recognize suspicious messages.
2. Increase the rate and speed of phishing reporting.
3. Identify gaps in awareness, process, and technical controls.
4. Reinforce safe behaviours without creating fear or blame.
5. Support continuous improvement of the security awareness program.
6. Help high-risk teams understand realistic social engineering risks.
7. Measure awareness maturity over time.

## Scope

Phishing simulations may apply to:

- All employees
- Contractors
- Temporary staff
- Executives
- High-risk business functions
- Finance teams
- HR teams
- IT and privileged users
- Customer support teams

The simulation scope should be approved before each campaign.

## Governance Requirements

Before any phishing simulation is launched, the organization should define and approve:

| Area | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Why the simulation is being conducted |
| Audience | Who will be included |
| Scenario | Type of phishing scenario to be used |
| Timing | When the simulation will run |
| Approval | Who has approved the activity |
| Data Handling | What data will be collected and how it will be protected |
| Reporting | How results will be reported |
| Follow-Up | What education or support will be provided |
| Exceptions | Any users, teams, or periods excluded from testing |

## Approval Process

Phishing simulations should be approved by relevant stakeholders before launch.

Recommended approvers include:

- Security or Cybersecurity Lead
- GRC or Compliance representative
- HR or People Team representative
- Legal or Data Protection representative where required
- Executive sponsor
- Communications team where internal messaging is required

## Ethical Principles

Phishing simulations must be ethical, proportionate, and educational.

The following principles should apply:

1. Do not publicly shame individuals.
2. Do not use simulations as disciplinary traps.
3. Do not target personal trauma, health scares, redundancy fears, or financial hardship.
4. Do not use themes that could cause unnecessary distress.
5. Do not collect passwords or sensitive personal information.
6. Do not run simulations without clear business justification.
7. Do not report results in a way that identifies individuals unnecessarily.
8. Provide learning support after the simulation.
9. Focus on behaviour improvement, not punishment.
10. Treat mistakes as learning opportunities.

## Simulation Design Rules

A phishing simulation should be realistic enough to be useful, but not harmful or manipulative.

Recommended design rules:

- Use workplace-relevant themes.
- Avoid extreme fear-based messages.
- Avoid sensitive personal topics.
- Avoid mimicking emergency, bereavement, health, or redundancy communications.
- Avoid collecting actual credentials.
- Avoid attachments that could be mistaken for real malware.
- Avoid scenarios that disrupt critical business operations.
- Clearly define success and failure criteria before launch.
- Ensure the reporting process is available and working.

## AI-Era Considerations

Modern phishing simulations should reflect the evolved threat landscape, including AI-enabled social engineering.

However, AI-era simulations should still remain ethical and controlled.

Relevant themes may include:

- Polished AI-generated emails with no spelling errors.
- Executive impersonation attempts.
- Supplier impersonation.
- Fake meeting invitations.
- Payment redirection attempts.
- Deepfake or voice-cloning awareness scenarios.
- Multi-channel social engineering awareness.
- Requests involving MFA codes or account recovery.

Employees should be taught that poor grammar is no longer a reliable phishing indicator. Attackers can now produce fluent, personalized, and convincing messages using AI tools.

## Deepfake and Voice-Cloning Simulation Guidance

If the organization chooses to include deepfake or voice-cloning awareness, the activity should be handled with extra care.

Guidance:

1. Obtain explicit senior approval.
2. Avoid using real employee voices or images without consent.
3. Do not create content that damages trust between staff and leadership.
4. Focus on verification behaviours, not deception.
5. Explain the learning purpose clearly after the exercise.
6. Use simulated examples or awareness demonstrations where possible.
7. Avoid targeting individuals with high-pressure scenarios.

Recommended learning outcome:

Employees should verify unusual requests through trusted channels, especially where the request involves money, access, credentials, confidential data, or process bypasses.

## Prohibited Simulation Themes

The following themes should not be used:

- Job loss or redundancy threats
- Health scares
- Death, bereavement, or family emergencies
- Personal financial hardship
- Immigration or legal threats
- Disciplinary action threats
- Highly sensitive HR matters
- Medical information
- Content that could cause distress or reputational harm
- Requests that encourage employees to reveal real passwords

## Data Protection and Privacy

Phishing simulation data should be handled carefully.

The organization should define:

- What data will be collected
- Who can access the data
- How long the data will be retained
- How results will be reported
- Whether results will be anonymized or aggregated
- Whether individual follow-up is required
- How data will be deleted after the retention period

Recommended practice:

- Report results at team or department level where possible.
- Limit access to individual-level results.
- Use individual results only for targeted support, not public criticism.
- Retain only the minimum data necessary.

## Metrics

Phishing simulation metrics should measure learning and behaviour improvement.

Recommended metrics include:

| Metric | Description |
|---|---|
| Reporting Rate | Percentage of users who reported the simulation message |
| Click Rate | Percentage of users who clicked the link |
| Submission Rate | Percentage of users who entered information, if safely simulated |
| Time to First Report | How quickly the first user reported the message |
| Repeat Susceptibility | Users or teams repeatedly interacting with simulations |
| Department-Level Trends | Comparison across business areas |
| Reporting Quality | Whether reports included useful context |
| Training Completion | Follow-up training completion after simulation |

## Metrics to Avoid Misusing

Avoid using metrics to create blame or fear.

Do not:

- Publish names of users who clicked.
- Rank employees publicly.
- Treat one click as misconduct.
- Use results without context.
- Ignore improvements in reporting behaviour.
- Focus only on click rates while ignoring reporting rates.

## Communication Plan

Before running simulations, organizations should decide whether employees will be generally informed that simulations may occur.

Recommended communication:

- Tell employees that phishing simulations are part of the awareness program.
- Explain that the purpose is learning and risk reduction.
- Reinforce that reporting is encouraged.
- Confirm that results will not be used for public shaming.
- Remind employees how to report suspicious messages.

## Post-Simulation Follow-Up

After a simulation, the organization should provide timely learning.

Follow-up may include:

- A short explanation of the simulation.
- Key warning signs from the scenario.
- What employees did well.
- How to report suspicious messages.
- What to do after clicking a suspicious link.
- Reminder that early reporting reduces harm.
- Optional micro-training for users who interacted with the simulation.

## Example Follow-Up Message

Use the message below after a simulation:

> Thank you to everyone who reported the recent phishing simulation. The purpose of this exercise was to help us strengthen our ability to recognize and report suspicious messages. If you clicked the message, please do not feel embarrassed. The important lesson is to pause, verify unusual requests, and report anything suspicious quickly. Early reporting helps protect the organization.

## Remediation and Support

Users who interact with phishing simulations should receive supportive follow-up.

Recommended actions:

- Assign short refresher training.
- Provide practical examples.
- Reinforce reporting routes.
- Offer team-level awareness sessions where needed.
- Review whether technical controls should be improved.
- Identify whether the scenario was too unclear or unrealistic.

## Frequency

Recommended frequency depends on organizational maturity and risk.

| Maturity Level | Suggested Frequency |
|---|---|
| Low maturity | 1 to 2 simulations per year |
| Developing maturity | Quarterly simulations |
| Mature program | Quarterly or risk-based simulations |
| High-risk teams | Additional targeted exercises where justified |

Simulations should not be so frequent that employees become fatigued or distrustful of internal communication.

## Roles and Responsibilities

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Security Team | Designs and manages the simulation |
| GRC / Compliance | Reviews governance, risk, and policy alignment |
| HR / People Team | Ensures ethical and fair treatment of employees |
| Legal / Privacy | Advises on legal and data protection considerations |
| Executive Sponsor | Approves and supports the program |
| Managers | Reinforce learning and encourage reporting |
| Employees | Report suspicious messages and complete assigned training |

## Simulation Lifecycle

A phishing simulation should follow this lifecycle:

1. Define objective.
2. Identify target audience.
3. Select scenario.
4. Review ethical and privacy considerations.
5. Obtain approval.
6. Confirm reporting channels are working.
7. Launch simulation.
8. Monitor results.
9. Provide learning feedback.
10. Analyse metrics.
11. Document lessons learned.
12. Update awareness content and controls.

## Best-Practice Summary

A well-governed phishing simulation should be:

- Approved
- Ethical
- Proportionate
- Educational
- Measurable
- Privacy-aware
- Risk-based
- Non-punitive
- Followed by learning
- Used to improve both people and controls

## Disclaimer

This document is a governance template and should be adapted to the organization’s legal, regulatory, cultural, and operational context. Organizations should consult relevant internal stakeholders before conducting phishing simulations.
